# Art and animation

Touchline Eleven uses original procedural assets. The JavaScript source is the editable asset source: no imported player likenesses, kit designs, stadium photographs, animations, logos, music or commercial advertising are included.

The v0.5 title screen uses the original, editable `assets/title-stadium.svg`: a blue stadium bowl, cyan floodlights and green pitch behind angular silver/white typography panels and restrained red accents. Its broadcast styling takes inspiration from the user's visual reference; it includes no copied photograph or external image asset. The illustration and styling are self-hosted. The opaque title screen suspends the unused 3D attract simulation and rendering.

`client/menu-theme.css` carries that palette through exhibition setup, online rooms, practice, settings, controls, pause, results and quitting. Setup and online screens share the illustrated stadium backdrop. Dialogs use deep blue panels, silver headings, cyan borders/focus/selection details and red primary actions. Team crests retain their identifying colours; successful practice outcomes retain green feedback. Narrow phone room controls wrap into readable rows, and short landscape pause menus use a compact action grid.

Menu actions have fully rounded ends, the side-selection cards have rounded corners, and pause/close icons are circular. Running uses a separate heel-recovery, forward-drive and landing sequence with opposing arm swing and a longer stride at speed. The free-running rig follows measured travel when velocity confirms movement; ball actions and close control keep their intended facing. Snapshot corrections to stationary players do not redirect the body. These changes affect presentation only.

`client/render.js` builds Meridian Ground: a 105 × 68 metre striped playing surface, 7.32 × 2.44 metre goal mouths, 16.5 metre penalty areas, 5.5 metre goal areas, 9.15 metre centre circle and penalty arcs, corner quadrants, four stepped stands, seat and spectator instances, cantilever roofs, four floodlight banks, dugouts, technical areas and original fictional pitchside typography. Canvas textures for turf, numbers, football panels, media boards and soft shadows are generated locally and do not require network requests.

Northstar Athletic wears deep blue with ivory shorts and a narrow ivory shirt stripe. Ember Borough wears amber with charcoal shorts and a charcoal chest band. Their keepers wear mint and lilac respectively. The controlled footballer has both a ground ring and an overhead triangle; an available receiver has an amber diamond, and a nearby switching candidate has a faint smaller diamond. Shirt numbers appear on the front and back.

`client/animation.js` defines approximately 1.8 metre tall articulated figures. A pelvis, torso, shoulders, elbows, hips and knees produce locomotion whose stride phase follows actual distance travelled, so idle figures do not walk in place. Approximate two-bone leg IK reaches cosmetic ankle targets, with world-space foot anchors during stance and boot counter-rotation to keep planted soles approximately parallel to the turf. Blended procedural poses cover passes, lofted passes, shots, standing tackles, sliding challenges and recovery, saves, contact and celebrations; dribble touch metadata guides the contacting foot. Numbered shirts share one texture atlas and one draw call. Seat, crowd-body and crowd-head geometry is instanced as well.

`client/player-model.js` supplies original curved profile meshes: an athletic chest and waist, sloped shoulders, tapered upper arms, forearms, thighs and calves, shaped hands and boots, a jaw and chin, ears, nose and facial details. Crop, swept and textured hair follow the scalp. Six skin tones and several hair/boot colours give the squads variation. A subtle generated fabric map, curved kit stripes, collar, sock bands and boot laces replace the old box-shaped detail. Shorts form one continuous garment, with two cosmetic hip-quaternion influences keeping the waist attached while the hems follow the legs. The same vertex deformation is used for their shadows. This is limited garment skinning, not a cloth simulation.

Player surfaces are instanced by shape and distance detail. A 22-metre entry / 25-metre exit threshold avoids repeated detail changes at the boundary. Nearby players have denser curved surfaces and small facial/accessory meshes; distant players preserve the silhouette and kit with fewer vertices and omit the small accessories. In the recorded broadcast fixture, all 22 models total 56,800 triangles across 18 player batches; the penalty fixture uses 69,800 triangles across 34 player batches with two nearby figures. These counts exclude the stadium and shadow rendering, and are not frame-rate claims. Both penalty and regular match cameras use this same model path.

Keeper presentation uses `client/keeper-animation.js` to anticipate the final part of an already released shot. A save turns and lifts the whole body, releases the foot plants and reaches the gloves toward the recorded contact. Catch and punch metadata come from real swept-contact outcomes in the shared simulation; the visual prediction cannot save a ball. Two-bone arm reaching places a caught ball between the rendered gloves, then brings it to the chest during recovery. Harder contacts retain the physical away-from-goal rebound and use a punching follow-through. Save recovery remains visible in the penalty camera, and practice advances neutral simulation during saved-shot feedback so catches, rebounds and landing do not freeze.

Animation remains a stylised approximation. The foot plants and IK are visual corrections on the flat pitch, not a physical contact or terrain-solving system; turns, pose blends and reach limits can still produce slipping or approximate contact. There is no motion capture, facial rig or cloth simulation. Authoritative ball release responds immediately; the visible kicking pose is a follow-through, so release and foot contact are approximate rather than exact physics-driven kicks. Save animation follows a reachable simulation save and never determines possession. Figures do not claim photorealistic or AAA character quality.

Broadcast and Telebroadcast keep a fixed sideline orientation: pitch +x remains screen-right, pitch −z remains screen-up through changes of ends. They track the ball with modest forward anticipation, bounded framing and adjustable height and zoom. Third person follows behind and above the selected player with the full avatar visible, configurable height, distance and field of view, and pointer/right-stick orbit. Movement follows the orbit direction; player switching retargets the follow camera. Stored First person preferences migrate to Third person. Reduced camera movement adjusts following, and setup menus use a wide stadium establishing view.

The shot guide combines a ground direction arrow with an intended target marker. Its target and power come from the same pure base-aim calculation as shot release; the simulation applies accuracy dispersion afterwards. The guide and existing shot-power bar are visual assistance, not a prediction of every bounce, interception or final shot result.

The pass receiver marker and dotted target line use `shared/passing.js` for the same intended receiver, lead and power as release. The meter changes between SHOT POWER, PASS POWER and THROUGH BALL without introducing another HUD panel. Guides and charge are hidden during menus, replay and practice feedback. Practice has a compact objective, attempt/success counters and outcome feedback instead of a match scorebug; normal player rendering, keeper saves and slide animations remain in use.

`client/replay.js` retains immutable snapshots in a bounded four-second history sampled at up to 20 Hz. An observed score increase can play the preceding three seconds in at most 2.6 seconds, using a temporary Telebroadcast camera during the existing goal break. Players and ball interpolate between snapshots, with a brief hold on the recorded goal endpoint. Skip returns to the current live view. Replays neither mutate authority nor consume gameplay random numbers, and cancel when live play leaves the goal phase. This is a short automatic goal replay, without a replay editor, saved video export or a freely controlled replay camera.

Low graphics cap render scale below device ratio 1, remove spectators and real shadow maps, and preserve all 22 figures and all simulation behaviour. Medium caps device ratio at 1.35 and uses 1024² shadows; high caps at 1.75 and uses 2048² shadows. Soft contact blobs remain available without real shadows. No post-processing chain or external texture download is required. Frame-rate figures in the verification report, when present, are measurements of the stated environment only.

Three.js is the external MIT-licensed rendering dependency; its licence is included by the build. All game-generated art is distributed under the project's source licence. Original procedural assets and their editable generators are bundled with the full source project.


Headers use a 0.64-second jump/recovery curve shared with the contact-height model, with raised feet, body lean and a head nod. Volleys lift and extend the striking leg while the other foot supports the player, with torso counterbalance and recovery. Both poses remain cosmetic: only the authoritative swept-contact calculation can change the ball. The inspected contact sheet shows eight phases of each animation.

Adaptive graphics retains the existing three detail presets, then scales the low-resolution back buffer to 80% or 65% of its usual dimensions under sustained load. UI text and touch controls remain at native CSS resolution. All players, ball physics and match timing remain unchanged. The touch layout editor uses the shared navy/cyan/red menu palette and rounded controls.

### Stadium sound

The visual assets remain original. Stadium crowd recordings are now self-hosted excerpts from Gregor Quendel's *Free Crowd Cheering Sounds* (CC BY 4.0). The source, licence and processing changes are credited in `assets/audio/CREDITS.md` and Settings. Contact effects, whistles, fallback ambience and the collective gasp remain original synthesis.
